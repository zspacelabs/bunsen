The bridge is where a recognizer's audio frames meet its text tokens. The four recognizers use four different bridges, and each one fixes how much audio each output step can see, what the decoder needs that a language model doesn't have, and whether the model can stream.

Four bridges

① Cross-attention (Whisper)

The Transformer's encoder–decoder bridge{{cite:vaswani2017}}: each decoder layer has a third sublayer whose queries come from the text and whose keys and values come from the encoder's output. Every text step can look at every frame of the window. The decoder is therefore not a plain language model: its layer has a sublayer the language kits lack, and its cache has two parts, self-attention keys that grow and cross-attention keys computed once per window.

② Splice (Qwen3-ASR)

The audio frames become ordinary positions in the decoder's input. A chat prompt holds a run of <|audio_pad|> placeholders, one per encoder frame, and the projected frames replace their embeddings. After that the decoder is an unmodified Qwen3: its self-attention reads audio and text alike, and the bridge has no parameters beyond the projector. The cost is that the audio occupies the context (13 positions a second).

③ Add (Voxtral Realtime)

The decoder takes one position per 80 ms of audio, and each position's input is the sum of a token's embedding and that 80 ms group's projected frames. Audio and text share positions rather than taking turns, so the context grows at 12.5 positions a second whatever is said, and each step sees only the audio up to now. That is what makes it stream.

④ Joint (Parakeet TDT)

A transducer has no attention between audio and text at all. A small prediction network summarizes the text so far, and the joint network adds its state to one encoder frame's and scores the next token (transducers). The decoder is cheap and frame-synchronous, at the cost of seeing one frame at a time (its encoder has already mixed context into every frame).

Delay and its conditioning

An additive bridge pairs each audio group with one output token, but a word can only be written once it has been heard. So Voxtral writes its transcript a fixed delay behind the audio: 480 ms by default, any multiple of 80 ms up to 1.2 s, or 2.4 s. A longer delay is more accurate and later. Because one checkpoint supports every delay, each layer is told which one it is running at: a sinusoidal embedding of the delay goes through a small MLP to a per-channel scale, 1 + f(delay), that multiplies the feed-forward's normalized input. That is the adaptive normalization of diffusion transformers{{cite:peebles2023}}, a FiLM-style conditioning{{cite:perez2018}} on a scalar.
